## Role in the poem

A suitor who bears the brunt of the fighting on the suitors' side and is killed by [Eumaeus](./eumaeus.md) in the hall.[^butler]

Butler's line — "Pisander, and Polybus son of Polyctor" — reads as though the patronymic belongs to Polybus. It does not: the preserved Greek reads Πείσανδρός τε Πολυκτορίδης Πόλυβός τε δαΐφρων, which makes [Pisander](./pisander.md) the son of Polyctor and gives Polybus only an epithet.[^greek] Book 18 confirms it in Butler's own English: "king Pisander son of Polyctor".[^butler] The comma is Butler's, and it has misled this wiki once already.

Butler's translation attaches the name to three unrelated men, and the distinction is easy to lose. Besides the suitor, there is a Polybus of Egyptian Thebes whose wife Alcandra gives [Helen](./helen.md) a silver work-box, and a Phaeacian craftsman who made the ball used in the dancing display before [Odysseus](./odysseus.md). The name also appears frequently as a patronymic that identifies someone else entirely: [Eurymachus](./eurymachus.md) is "son of Polybus" throughout the Ithacan books.[^butler]

The necklace given to [Penelope](./penelope.md) among the suitors' gifts is not his: Butler assigns it to [Pisander](./pisander.md) son of Polyctor.[^butler]

## Where to read

Book 22, for the suitor himself. Book 4 names the Egyptian Polybus and Book 8 the Phaeacian craftsman.
