## Four screen strategies

Mario Camerini's *Ulysses* (1954), Jean-Luc Godard's *Contempt* (1963), the 1968 RAI miniseries, and Andrei Konchalovsky's 1997 television production represent sharply different adaptation strategies. The local [adaptation survey record](../external-sources/history-adaptations.md) preserves none of the article's text, so it cannot support the descriptions below; of these four productions it lists only *Contempt*. The other three are identified here from the films themselves, and readers who want a source should go to the works.[^adapt] A feature film compresses; a miniseries can preserve episodes; a modernized analogue replaces literal correspondences with patterns; meta-cinema can make the failure to adapt part of its subject.

## What the camera changes

Film turns narrated marvel into visible bodies and landscapes. That raises practical and interpretive choices: whether monsters should appear fantastic or psychological, whether gods act visibly, and whether Odysseus's retrospective tale is presented as memory, testimony, or objective event.

Performance redistributes sympathy. A pause from Penelope, Telemachus, Circe, or a crewman can create interiority that the camera cannot attribute to an epic narrator. Editing also controls recognition: viewers may know a disguised figure before another character does, producing suspense through unequal knowledge.
