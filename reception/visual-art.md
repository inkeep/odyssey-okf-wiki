## The chosen instant

A visual work cannot reproduce the whole temporal movement of an episode at once. It selects an instant: the approach to the Cyclops, the Sirens beside the ship, Nausicaa before the stranger, recognition at the bed, or violence in the hall. What appears before and after must be supplied by the viewer.

The Metropolitan Museum record for the 1806–13 Pope edition documents a collaborative object involving translation, illustration, and engraving, crediting a translator and six illustrators, along with engravers the record lists only in part.[^met] Illustrated books therefore adapt twice: verbal translation reshapes Homer, then images select and frame that translated narrative.

## Questions for an image

- Which instant is shown, and which consequence is withheld?
- Does Odysseus appear as thinker, sufferer, warrior, voyeur, or storyteller?
- Are monsters treated as bodies, landscapes, allegories, or decorative motifs?
- Where does the viewer stand relative to danger and desire?
- Do costume and architecture claim antiquarian accuracy or contemporary relevance?

## Series and single images

A sequence can create its own canon by repeating some episodes and omitting others. A single iconic image can detach a scene from its ethical context: the Sirens may become generalized temptation, while the crew's labor and Odysseus's controlled exposure disappear. Visual reception is therefore evidence about what an era found memorable as well as a new interpretation of the poem.[^adapt]
